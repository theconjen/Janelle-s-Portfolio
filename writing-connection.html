Writing

A different kind of room.

The essay I published when I launched The Connection — on digital loneliness, and why I built a social platform with no ads and no algorithms.

There is a certain shade of loneliness that can only be found in a crowd. I think we’ve all felt it. It’s the quiet ache of being surrounded by people but profoundly unseen, the digital equivalent of standing in the fluorescent hum of a bus station at midnight, watching faces pass under the clock without a single one turning to meet your eyes. For me, that feeling had begun to define my experience of being a Christian online.

I would scroll through feeds populated with ghosts and echoes of fellowship. There was no shortage of content, of course. There never is. I saw scripture verses laid over watercolor sunsets, crisp and beautiful and utterly disconnected from the person who posted them. I watched videos of sermons that were powerful for a moment and then vanished, replaced by a cooking tutorial or a political rant. I saw prayer requests float through direct messages like notes in a bottle, cast into a digital ocean with a hope that they might wash up on a friendly shore. The internet was a torrent of Christian information, a library of Alexandria for apologetics and theology, but it felt like a library with no talking allowed. The one thing I could never seem to find was a front porch. A living room. A place where the Body of Christ could do more than just broadcast at one another.

We were all there, millions of us, logged into the same handful of platforms. We were all sharing, liking, and commenting, performing the rituals of connection without the substance of it. But we weren't really meeting. We weren't learning each other's names, hearing the particular cadence of each other's voices, or seeing the exhaustion and the hope that lives behind the eyes. We weren't bearing one another's burdens so much as we were acknowledging them with a quick tap of a heart icon before the algorithm served up the next distraction. We were a congregation scattered across a field of glass, each of us staring at a reflection of community instead of seeing the real thing.

The problem, I came to realize, was architectural. These digital spaces were never built to hold a church. They were built to hold our attention. Their foundations are laid not for fellowship, but for commerce. Their walls are designed not to create intimacy, but to generate traffic. Every feature, from the endless scroll that keeps our thumbs moving to the notifications that jolt us with a hit of social validation, is engineered to keep us consuming. In this environment, the loudest, most shocking, or most polished content naturally rises to the top. Thoughtful, patient, and gentle conversation sinks. Virality becomes the measure of worth. Faithfulness, which is quiet and slow and often hidden, doesn't register. It can’t be measured, so it doesn't count. We were all trying to have a quiet, holy encounter in the middle of a casino, and we were wondering why we couldn’t hear God's still, small voice over the clatter of the slot machines.

These places are not built for us to love one another.

For years, I just accepted this as a non-negotiable reality of modern life. We were all aware of the deficit. We felt the spiritual friction, the strange emptiness after an hour of scrolling through what was supposed to be uplifting content. We complained about the arguments in the comments, the performative piety, the feeling of being sold to even in our most sacred spaces. But the platforms were the public square. They were where the people were. It seemed there was no alternative, so we stayed, hoping we could somehow carve out a holy corner on land that was owned and designed by someone else. We were trying to grow a garden on an asphalt parking lot.

Then one evening last year, it became too much. I sat in the dark, the blue light of my phone painting my face, and felt a profound sense of grief. I was looking at a photo from a friend’s small group, and my first thought wasn't joy for them, but a pang of longing for that kind of simple, embodied presence. The discontent had been simmering for years, but on that night it boiled over into a kind of conviction. It felt less like an idea and more like a command. The question that had been a faint whisper for so long — “What if there were a better way?” — became a thunderous and personal calling. If the places we have don’t work, then we must build new ones. It was a terrifying thought, filled with the certainty of my own inadequacy. Who was I to build an ark?

So last year, I began to build a different kind of room.

I called it The Connection. It isn't another feed to scroll through. It is a platform, yes, but I think of it more as a quiet collection of interlocking rooms, like a well-loved parish hall or a home with its doors thrown open. It was designed from the ground up with a single purpose: to give believers a place to actually find and know each other.

It begins with Communities. These are not massive, anonymous groups. They are focused spaces for people to gather around a shared calling, a common interest, or a specific season of life. We already have a community for Christian artists sharing their work and encouraging each other through creative blocks. There’s a group for parents of children with special needs, which has become a sanctuary for late-night prayers and raw, honest support. Another small band is reading through Dorothy Sayers' letters and discussing her fierce intellect and faith. These are places to go deeper with a dedicated few, not wider with a disconnected many.

From those communities, life spills into Events. We built a simple tool for people to host their own gatherings, and not just the kind of passive webinars where one person talks and everyone else listens. A woman in Tennessee hosts a weekly Lectio Divina on Thursday mornings, where a handful of people from four different states read scripture together and simply listen. A men’s group from a church in Oregon uses it for their Saturday morning check-in, sharing coffee over video before their kids wake up. These are moments of real-time presence, a digital incarnation of breaking bread together. They are small acts of showing up.

I also knew that any honest Christian space had to make room for doubt. Faith is not the absence of questions; it is the courage to live within them. So, we built an apologetics Library, but not as an arsenal of debate tactics. It's a resource room for the soul. It's for when you're hit with the staggering reality of the world's pain and you find yourself wrestling with the same questions that troubled Job. It’s for when a skeptical friend asks a hard question about the historicity of the Resurrection and you realize you don’t have a ready answer. It's a quiet place to sit with the thoughts of thinkers like Tim Keller or N.T. Wright and find solid ground for your feet.

Perhaps the heart of The Connection, though, is the Prayer Wall. This is where the abstract idea of community becomes breathtakingly specific. It’s not a place for vague requests. It's a place for names. It is for “Please pray for my mother, Sarah, who is starting chemotherapy on Tuesday. Pray for her peace and for the hands of her doctors.” It is for “We are celebrating the birth of our son, Leo. Thank you, God.” It is for “I am wrestling with a season of depression and feel completely alone.” To see someone’s name, to type it out in your own prayer, is a sacred act. It resists the anonymity of the internet. It is a promise to carry someone, by name, before the throne of grace.

All of this is tied together with a simple Chat that connects you directly with the people you are actually doing life with. The people in your specific small group, the people who attended that event with you. It is the digital equivalent of catching up in the church narthex after the service, not a cold message from a stranger you have nothing in common with.

Two things are completely absent from this platform. You will find no advertisements. And you will find no hidden algorithms. This is not a minor detail. It is the entire point. An algorithm is a silent, invisible curator of your spiritual life. Its code is not written to bring you closer to God; it is written to provoke a reaction, to keep your eyes on the screen. The easiest human reactions to provoke are outrage, envy, and anxiety. The fruits of the spirit — love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, and self-control — do not generate clicks. They do not go viral. They are quiet miracles.

We cannot serve both God and an algorithm.

Likewise, the business model of the entire social internet is built on advertising. And the business of advertising is the manufacturing of discontent. Every ad you see is a subtle whisper that you are not enough. Your life is not full enough, your home is not clean enough, your body is not right enough, your family is not happy enough. The Gospel is a declaration that in Christ, you are already whole. You are beloved, redeemed, and complete. These two messages cannot live in the same house. One will inevitably poison the other. The Connection is a quiet space, free from the constant hum of manufactured desire.

I launched it on the App Store earlier this year, with very little fanfare. I didn't run a single paid ad. I just told a few friends, who told a few more. In the months since, over a thousand people have downloaded it. That number is gratifying, but it's not the thing that makes me feel like this work matters.

The true miracle is this: they stayed. They came, they saw something that felt different, and they began to put down roots. A youth pastor from a rural part of Nebraska brought his entire youth group on, giving them a safe place to talk that isn’t littered with distractions. A Bible study that had been meeting on a chaotic group text moved their conversations and their prayer requests into a private community. I see friendships forming between a woman in Seattle and a man in Florida because they discovered a shared love for the writings of the desert fathers. The average person who joins comes back multiple times a week. They are not just consuming. They are contributing, sharing, and building.

If this longing for a more genuine, more human-scaled Christian community resonates with you, I would love for you to see what we're building. If you've been looking for a place to gather your own people, away from all the noise and the selling, this might be the tool you’ve been hoping for.

You can download The Connection from the App Store.

Whatever weighs on your heart tonight, someone here wants to help you carry it.

Janelle
