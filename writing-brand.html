The brand is not the paint.

Why brand work has to come first — lessons on language, product, and belonging from building at Bord Labs and The Connection.

You think the brand is the paint. A final, decorative coat you apply to the house once the foundation is poured, the walls are framed, and the plumbing is set. It’s the satisfying, colorful work done at the very end, when the difficult, serious business of construction is complete. You see it as the choice of Helvetica versus Garamond, the Pantone swatch for the CTA button, the specific flavor of upbeat sincerity you bake into your website’s microcopy. Polish. A surface treatment.

This is a quiet and common way to guarantee your own failure. It is the single most prevalent mistake founders make, a subtle error in chronology that sends fractures through everything that follows. You are treating the very soul of your company as an afterthought.

A brand is not a coat of paint. It is the architectural blueprint. It is the foundational decision that dictates the shape of the floor plan, the load-bearing capacity of the support beams, and the precise angle at which the morning light will enter the kitchen. It is the operating logic that precedes the product, that informs the marketing, that filters the candidates you hire. Your brand determines which version of the future you are building, which people will show up to live in it, and whether they see any reason to stay. It is the first thing, not the last. It is the cause, not the effect.

Every other decision you make flows downstream from this one. And the quality of that first decision sets the absolute ceiling for everything else you hope to achieve.

Your Words Build Your World

Let's begin with the language you use. Not the words in your advertisements, but the words you use in your own private meetings when you describe what you are building. This internal vocabulary is the first product you ship. When I was building at a company called Bord Labs, we consciously retired the dead language of B2B software. We purged the lexicon of acronyms and feature-speak that plagues the industry — the soulless procession of terms like “AI SDR,” “GTM automation,” and “ICP tooling.”

This dialect is fluent only to sales operations managers and venture capitalists. It is a foreign tongue to the person you are supposedly serving: the founder. So we made a choice. We would speak their native language. The tagline that emerged from this was, “Build the company. We’ll handle the room.”

That decision echoed backward through the entire organization. It wasn’t a marketing flourish added after the fact; it was a constitutional amendment that changed what the product team built next. The moment you commit to speaking a founder’s language, you instinctively stop building features that only make sense on a spreadsheet. You stop adding another dial to a dashboard nobody looks at. You begin, instead, to build the tools a founder would actually ask for if they could articulate the shape of their own desperation. You start solving the problems that keep them awake at night, not the problems that help a middle manager justify their budget.

Brand, when taken seriously, is a brutal and effective filter for your product roadmap. It is a constant, nagging question you must ask of every proposed feature, of every new line of code: does this honor our promise? Does this sound like us? Does this feel like something we would make? A brand is a theory of the world. Your product is the evidence you present to prove that theory is correct. If your theory is generic, your evidence will be forgettable. The language you choose is the first and most telling piece of evidence you will ever offer. It silently communicates who you are for, and in doing so, declares who you are not for. That act of exclusion is the beginning of all belonging.

This isn’t just about taglines. It’s about the very name on the door. Consider the name “Stripe.” It suggests something clean, fundamental, a direct line. It carries an implicit promise of simplicity in a world of convoluted payment gateways. Compare that to the enterprise graveyards filled with names that sound like pharmaceutical side effects or defense contractors. Those names also make a promise: a promise of complexity, of bureaucracy, of a sales process that will grind you into dust. Your name is a story in a single word. It should be a story your intended user wants to be a part of.

The words you use internally become the culture. The words you use externally become the perception. A strong brand aligns these two, so that what you believe inside is what people experience outside. When your team talks about users as “leads” or “targets,” they will build a product that feels like a trap. When they talk about them as members, guests, or patrons, they will build a product that feels like a home.

That choice of language is a branding decision. It likely costs nothing. And it changes everything.

The Product as a Kept Promise

The product itself is nothing more than the physical manifestation of your brand’s core promise. Every button, every workflow, every pixel is either a confirmation or a betrayal of that promise. Your job, as the founder, is to be the chief arbiter of this consistency. You are not just overseeing the creation of a tool; you are directing the fulfillment of a prophecy.

Think of the first iPod. The brand promise was not “a portable media player with a 5GB Toshiba hard drive and a FireWire port.” The promise was, famously, “1,000 songs in your pocket.” It was a promise about feeling, about liberation, about a new kind of personal freedom. The product that Apple delivered was a perfect expression of that promise. The click wheel was not merely a clever user interface. It was a physical rejection of the fussy, button-heavy complexity of every other MP3 player on the market. It was simplicity made tangible. The stark white earbuds weren’t just a design choice; they were a public signal of belonging to a new tribe.

Every part of the experience, from the satisfying heft of the device in your hand to the clean animation of the screen, was a confirmation of the brand’s core tenets: simplicity, elegance, and human-centered design. A competitor could easily copy the hard drive capacity or the file format support. But they could not copy the feeling. The feeling was the product of a brand decision made years before the first unit ever shipped.

Now, apply this scrutiny to your own work. If your brand promises “effortless collaboration,” does your product require a three-hour onboarding session? If your brand is about “radical transparency,” are your pricing plans hidden behind a “Contact Sales” form? These are not small inconsistencies. They are lies. And your users will feel them on a chemical level. They may not be able to articulate why your product feels wrong, but they will know it. They will feel the gap between the story you told them and the reality you delivered.

This is why brand work must happen first. It gives you a constitution. It is the document against which all future product decisions must be judged. You, the founder, become the guardian of this document. Your role shifts from being the person with all the feature ideas to being the person who says “no” to most of them. You become an editor, and the brand is your style guide. You are no longer just asking, “Is this feature possible?” You are asking a much harder set of questions. Does this belong? Does this simplify or complicate? Does this serve our truest believer or a hypothetical edge case? Does this feel like us?

A product without a brand is an orphan. It has no lineage, no guiding principles. It will drift with the winds of customer requests and competitor features, bloating into a Frankenstein’s monster of disconnected functions. It will do many things, but it will stand for nothing. A product born from a strong brand knows exactly what it is. It has a point of view. It is willing to be disliked by some in order to be loved by others. It is an opinion, rendered in code.

A Congregation of Believers

Users are a metric. An audience is a commodity. A congregation is a force of nature. A powerful brand does not just acquire users; it gathers a congregation. It draws people together who see the world the way you do, who believe what you believe. These people are not just customers. They are apostles.

I saw this firsthand with a platform I built called The Connection. It was created for a specific community: Christians who wanted a digital space free from the corrosive dynamics of mainstream social media. The brand promise was explicit and uncompromising: “Built for believers. No ads. No algorithms.” It was a statement of values disguised as a feature list.

We achieved over a thousand organic downloads with no paid marketing. But that number is a vanity metric. The number that mattered was retention. The people who joined stayed. They did not churn. More than that, they started to proselytize. They brought their small groups, their Bible studies, their youth ministries onto the platform. The product did not spread because it was technically superior to Facebook Groups or Discord. It spread because it made a promise that resonated with a specific group of people, and the product experience was an unbroken continuation of that promise.

The promise of “no ads” meant their attention would never be sold. The promise of “no algorithms” meant their relationships would never be manipulated for engagement. It created a high-trust environment by defining itself against the low-trust world of existing social networks. A competitor could replicate the features in a weekend. They could build a better group chat or a slicker event calendar. But they could not replicate the trust. The trust was the brand.

This is the power of creating what I call “brand gravity.” Most companies operate with a weak gravitational field. They have to spend enormous amounts of money on paid acquisition, constantly fighting the entropy of the market, pulling users into their orbit one by one through sheer force of ad spend. Your customer acquisition cost is a direct tax on the weakness of your brand.

A strong brand, however, has its own gravity. It pulls people in. They don’t just stumble upon your product; they seek it out. They hear about it from a friend, in a community forum, in a podcast that aligns with their values. They arrive already believing. They come not as skeptical consumers but as hopeful pilgrims looking for a place to belong. When you get this right, your users stop being a line item on a spreadsheet and become your most potent and passionate sales force. They don't just use your product; they identify with it. Using your product becomes a small act of self-expression, a signal to others about who they are and what they value.

This requires courage. The courage to be specific. To build a cathedral for a hundred true believers rather than a warehouse for a million casual visitors. A brand is defined just as much by who it excludes as by who it includes. That promise of “built for believers” was a deliberate act of exclusion. It signaled to a vast majority of the market that this product was not for them. And in doing so, it created an intense, magnetic pull for the minority it was designed to serve. The fear of missing out on the total addressable market is what drives founders to build generic, soulless products for everyone and no one.

Choose your congregation. Make them a promise. And then spend the rest of your company’s life keeping it.

The Alchemy of Imperfection

There is a moment in the life of every new company where the reality of the product is embarrassingly far from the grandeur of the vision. Your minimum viable product is often just that: minimum. It’s buggy. It’s missing features your users consider basic. It crashes at inconvenient times.

Without a brand, these flaws are just flaws. They are evidence of your amateurism. They are reasons for your users to churn and to tell their friends you are not ready for prime time. They are liabilities.

With a strong brand, you can perform a kind of alchemy. You can transform liabilities into assets. The bugs become quirks, endearing signs of an authentic, early-stage product. A missing feature isn’t an oversight; it’s a mark of your disciplined, minimalist focus. The slow load times are a small price to pay for being part of something new and important. Your brand provides the narrative context that reframes imperfection.

Think of the first-generation Tesla Roadster. By any objective measure, it was a deeply flawed product. It was wildly expensive, based on the chassis of a Lotus Elise, had a limited and often unpredictable range, and was plagued with reliability issues. If it had been released by General Motors, it would have been dismissed as a catastrophic failure, a laughingstock.

But it wasn't. It was released by Tesla, a company whose brand was forged from the myth of a maverick inventor, a defiant stand against a complacent auto industry, and a mission to accelerate the world’s transition to sustainable energy. The brand was so potent that it changed the very nature of the product’s failures. Owners didn’t feel like they had purchased a faulty car; they felt like they were patrons of a revolution. They were co-conspirators in a grand project. The long waiting list, the production delays, the software glitches — all of it became part of the lore. It was proof that they were on the front lines of the future, enduring the small hardships of the pioneer.

This is an act of narrative jujitsu that is impossible without a powerful brand. A brand gives you air cover. It allows you to ship an imperfect product that people will love not in spite of its flaws, but because of what those flaws represent. It buys you time. Time to fix the bugs, to build the missing features, to scale the infrastructure.

Your MVP is never just about the product. It is a test of your brand’s ability to create desire and forgive imperfection. Many founders mistakenly believe they must build a perfect product before they can build a brand. The truth is the inverse. You must build a compelling brand so that you can get away with shipping an imperfect product. The brand is the story that makes the scarcity of your early offering feel exclusive, not insufficient. It’s what allowed a company like Superhuman to turn a waitlist, a technical solution to scaling problems, into one of the most effective marketing instruments of the last decade. The brand made waiting feel like a privilege.

Your brand is your shield during the vulnerable early days. It protects you from the justified criticisms of a product that is not yet fully formed. It gives your earliest adopters a reason to believe in the product you’ve promised, not just the one you’ve managed to ship.

The Story That Never Ends

A launch is a spike. A lifecycle is a curve. We live in a culture obsessed with the launch: the TechCrunch headline, the Product Hunt sweep, the sudden rush of day-one signups. Founders burn themselves out optimizing for this single moment, this ephemeral burst of attention. And then, a few months later, they stare at a flattening engagement chart and wonder what went wrong.

What went wrong is they mistaken a firework for a star. A launch is a moment. A brand is a continuum. A weak brand can generate a launch; the novelty of a new thing is often enough to capture eyeballs for a week. But only a strong brand can generate a lifecycle. Only a strong brand can make the eighteenth month of your company’s life as exciting as the first.

What sustains engagement over the long term is not a relentless firehose of new content or an ever-expanding list of features. What sustains engagement is a feeling that something is happening. It is the steady, unfolding narrative of your brand. It is the user’s belief that they are part of a story that is going somewhere interesting. This is what turns a one-time transaction into a long-term relationship.

This narrative momentum is built from consistency. Consistent visual identity, yes. But more profoundly, a consistent voice and a consistent point of view. It’s the feeling that there is an intelligent, opinionated human presence behind the curtain. It’s the reassurance that the company still believes the same things it did when you first signed up. This consistency doesn't mean you can't evolve. It means you evolve like a character in a novel — you grow and change, but you remain recognizably yourself.

A brand with narrative momentum gives people a reason to check back in. It creates an anticipation for what you will say or do next. The outdoor apparel company Patagonia has mastered this. People don't just buy their jackets; they follow the company. They wait to see what bold environmental stance it will take, what anti-consumerist statement it will make in its next ad campaign, what groundbreaking material science it will announce. The product is excellent, but the brand’s story is what keeps people engaged between purchases. It has created a franchise, not just a clothing line.

You must think of your company not as a machine for shipping features, but as a platform for telling a story. Every blog post, every email, every product update, every tweet is another chapter. Does it advance the plot? Does it deepen the character? Does it stay true to the central themes?

The mistake is to see these things as “marketing content” to be churned out. They are not. They are the living expression of your brand. They are the work that makes your product more than a piece of disposable utility. A founder who understands this stops asking, “What can we ship to re-engage our users?” and starts asking, “What can we do to remind our users what we stand for?”

The first question leads to feature bloat and desperate, pleading push notifications.

The second leads to loyalty.

The First Decision

So you are about to begin. You have the idea, the restless energy, the blank whiteboard. Before you draw the first box in your system architecture diagram, before you write the first line of code, before you court the first dollar of investment, you must stop.

You must answer the first and most important questions.

Who are you for? Be specific enough to be wrong.

What wound in the world do you exist to heal?

What is the singular promise you can make to those people that no one else can? A promise you are willing to stake your company’s life on.

This is the brand work. It is not choosing a color. It is not designing a logo. It is the act of deciding who you are. This work is quiet, difficult, and receives no external validation. It is the lonely work of the founder. But it is the only work that gives meaning to all the frantic activity that will follow.

The brand is not the decoration you apply at the end. It is the foundation upon which the entire structure rests. It is the decision from which all other decisions are born. Get it right, and it will guide you. Get it wrong, and you will spend years fighting against the current of your own creation.

Do this work first. Do it seriously. Your company’s survival depends on it.
